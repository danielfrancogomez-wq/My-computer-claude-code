If she pushes back

Three questions a sharp client — or a marker — could reasonably ask. Have these ready; don't volunteer them.

"Why are the running shoes an asset and not just spending?"

Because they're sporting equipment you still own at month end — the same logic that puts your camping and sports gear on the balance sheet in March.

If you'd rather treat them as everyday spending, assets rise $2,009 instead of $2,261 and expenses become $632 instead of $380. The equation still balances either way; the point is to be consistent, and we've been consistent with March.

"Where does the $53,864 opening net worth come from?"

It's what you must have been worth on 1 March for the month to end where it did.

Working backwards: $56,958 closing, less the $3,094 you earned, equals $53,864. It also reconciles forwards — your assets at 1 March were $56,979, and you owed $3,115 at that point, including a credit card balance you'd only partly cleared.

"Why is a gift income? I didn't earn it."

Income isn't defined by effort — it's anything that increases your net worth without you borrowing to get it.

The gift card made you $100 better off, so it's revenue. But it never went through your bank, so it's deliberately left out of the cash flow statement. Same reasoning as the casino win in April.
